# KAGE

*Korean Age, and the pressure to belong*

August 2025

A country can leave you amazed and uneasy at the same time. Korea has done both. There is much to admire: the energy of its cities, the care visible in ordinary details, and the way something unfamiliar can become part of everyday life. Living here has made the world feel larger. It has also made certain questions harder to ignore.

The most persistent one is about belonging. At what point does learning how to live among others become learning how to disappear into them?

KAGE is the name I give that tension. It brings together K and age: Korean Age, a name for this period of looking at life in Korea from the outside. Its echo of cage carries another meaning. The concern is not a literal enclosure, but the possibility of a life shaped so carefully around expectations that leaving them begins to feel impossible.

From the outside, social life can sometimes look like a performance whose rules everyone else already knows. There seems to be a right way to appear composed, to respond, to present ambition, to avoid making a moment uncomfortable. A conversation may feel warm while leaving little room for anything untidy. What stays with me is the distance between being seen and being allowed to be fully present.

That distance is difficult to read fairly. A reserved response may be courtesy rather than coldness. Agreement may protect a relationship rather than conceal a belief. What looks like a facade to a newcomer may carry meanings that become clearer with time. Being outside a culture offers a different angle; it does not automatically offer a better understanding.

Still, the feeling remains. In some spaces, the polished surface seems to matter so much that uncertainty has to be hidden before it can be spoken. Everyone appears to understand the presentation, and everyone helps keep it intact. The performance becomes convincing because it is shared. Eventually, it can become difficult to tell whether anyone still feels free to step away from it.

A mask is not always dishonest. It can protect privacy, keep a difficult day from spilling into someone else's, or make a fragile interaction possible. The pressure begins when wearing it stops feeling like a choice. When composure becomes compulsory, even ordinary sadness or hesitation can seem like a failure to behave correctly.

This is where fascination turns into a more personal fear. There is a difference between wanting to understand a place and wanting its approval so badly that every part of yourself becomes negotiable. Learning the language, showing respect, and adjusting to unfamiliar customs can open a life. Constantly checking whether your personality is acceptable can make that life smaller.

The temptation is easy to understand. Fitting in promises relief from being noticeable, from explaining yourself, from wondering whether you have misunderstood the room. An outsider can grow tired of being outside. The danger is accepting every expectation in exchange for that relief, until belonging depends on maintaining a version of yourself you can no longer put down.

Then the cage would no longer be something observed in other people. It would be a habit carried into each conversation: choosing the safe answer, hiding the awkward truth, appearing certain when certainty is absent. The door might remain open, but the thought of disappointing someone could be enough to keep it closed.

None of this describes all Koreans, or every part of life here. There have been encounters that felt generous, direct, and free of the pressure described above. Nor does Korea own the habit of performance. Other societies have their own ways of rewarding appearances. Being here has simply brought this tension into sharper focus, perhaps partly because unfamiliar expectations are easier to notice than the ones a person grows up accepting.

It would be unfair to turn that tension into a verdict on a country. It is more useful as a question about how to live within it. Can admiration leave room for discomfort? Can adaptation happen without surrendering the parts of a person that make connection honest? Can belonging make space for someone who has not mastered the performance?

There is no wish to remain a permanent spectator. Distance can become its own comfortable mask. A meaningful life here requires participation, patience, and the willingness to be corrected. But it should also leave room to speak imperfectly, to disagree gently, and to be present without looking entirely composed.

Korea continues to fascinate me. The question is how to move closer without mistaking approval for connection. Perhaps that is what KAGE names most clearly: the hope of belonging somewhere without making a cage of the effort to belong.
